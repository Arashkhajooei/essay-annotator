RITA Annotation Rules

Tag by what the text does in its paragraph, not by its words. One sentence can hold two tags.

The 8 codes — grouped by where they happen

1 Introduction — how the essay opens
LED Lead
Hook that grabs attention, takes no side yet.
POS Position
The writer's overall stance on the prompt.
The thesis — just one
↓
2 Body paragraphs — the argument is built here
CLM Claim
A reason that supports the Position.
EVD Evidence
Facts, examples, anecdotes (personal examples), source material, elaboration, citation.
Backs a claim
CTR Counterclaim
A reason for the opposing side.
REB Rebuttal
Attacks the Counterclaim.
Needs a CTR first
↓
3 Conclusion — the last paragraph
CON Conclusion
Restates the position and claims.
UNA Unannotated
No argumentative work — transitions, filler. Can appear anywhere.
Key rules
1. Lead is the opening hook. Does it grab attention and take no side? Mark it as Lead. It usually appears in the first or second sentence of the writing.
2. A restated prompt is not a Lead. Ask: did the writer invent this to hook me, or just echo the prompt? An invented hook is Lead. Just echoing the prompt question is Unannotated.
3. Lead ends where the stance begins. Lead ends the moment a stance appears. Split the sentence: the hook is Lead, the stance is Position.
4. One position per essay. Every essay states its position once, in the introduction or opening lines. That is the thesis. If the same stance appears again later in restated or paraphrased form, do not tag a second Position. A restated position in the conclusion is simply the Conclusion.
5. "It depends" is still a Position. Sometimes the writer does not pick a firm yes or no. They say "it depends" or "sometimes yes, sometimes no." That is still their Position. Tag it as Position.
6. Claim or Evidence? A Claim is something you can argue. Evidence is something you can check. Both in one sentence? Tag two.
Example: "The internet makes life easier" is a Claim. "Over 5 billion people use the internet" is Evidence.
7. What counts as Evidence. Is it a fact, an example, mentioned source material, detail and elaboration, or a citation? All of these are tagged as Evidence.
8. Evidence attaches to a claim. Evidence with no explicit claim attaches to the nearest Claim. If none is present, attach it to the Position.
9. Each claim is unique. An essay can have many claims, but each is a distinct point made once. If you see a claim repeated, paraphrased, or restated elsewhere, do not tag it as a new Claim. Mark the repeat as Unannotated.
10. Tag each point only once. When you tag a Claim, Counterclaim, or Position, tag it only once, where it first appears. If the writer says that same idea again later, even in different words, do not give it a new tag. Leave the repeat as Unannotated. Only a genuinely new point gets its own tag.
11. You see a claim/reason? check the side. Whose side is it on? The writer's side is a Claim. The opposing side, not the writer's, is a Counterclaim.
12. Does it reply to the opposing side? A sentence that answers a Counterclaim and pushes back against it is a Rebuttal. It always comes right after a Counterclaim.
Rebuttal attacks a Counterclaim.
13. Rebuttal only weakens the counterclaim. A fresh reason for the writer's own side is a Claim, not a Rebuttal, even when it sits right after a Counterclaim.
14. Not every Counterclaim needs a Rebuttal. A Counterclaim can stand alone. If the writer raises the other side but never answers it, leave it as a Counterclaim only.
15. Conclusions are wrap-ups. Predictions, rhetorical questions, and recommendations in the closing lines add no new argument. They are just wrap-up, so keep them Unannotated. Only the restated position and claims in the final paragraph are tagged as Conclusion.
16. No rhetorical or argumentative role? If a span does no argumentative work, connectors, filler, a restated prompt, or a repeat, mark it as Unannotated.

How the parts connect

Parent → Child
The claim supports the position. Position → Claim
The counterclaim argues against the position. Position → Counterclaim
The evidence backs up the claim. Claim → Evidence
The rebuttal answers the counterclaim. Counterclaim → Rebuttal
The evidence backs up the counterclaim. Counterclaim → Evidence
The evidence backs up the rebuttal. Rebuttal → Evidence
Each box sits inside its parent. A box inside another means it supports or answers the one around it.
